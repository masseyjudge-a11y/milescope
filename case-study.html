Points are only worth something if you can use them.

01 · The problem

Your points can go almost anywhere. That’s what makes them hard to use.

Chase, Amex, Capital One, Citi and Bilt points can each move to a dozen or more airlines, at different rates, and the bonuses change every few weeks. The same flight can cost 52,500 miles through one airline and 88,000 through another. Figuring out the best option means juggling transfer charts, award charts and seat searches across a lot of tabs.

Who it’s for

People with two or more points cards

They take one to three trips a year on points. They know points are worth more than cash back, but not how to get that value on a specific trip.

What they want

“Show me where I can go, and the cheapest way to pay.”

Other tools each answer part of that. Award search assumes you already know the airline, card sites only show their own points, and blogs talk about deals in general, not your balances.

02 · Testing my first version

Version 1 looked done. When I checked it properly, it wasn’t something you could trust.

Before adding anything new, I went through it like a skeptical user: checked every airline and transfer partner against the real charts, and ran through setup from start to finish. If an app like this is wrong even once, people stop using it, so fixing accuracy came first.

04 · Decisions I made

What I picked, what I gave up, and why.

Prices

Search live seats for every routeMost accurate, but slow and expensive.
Estimates first, live check when you askBrowsing is instant, and real seats load when you’re ready to book.

Why: browsing needs to be fast, booking needs to be right. Every estimate is labeled as one.

Accounts

Sign up to save your walletSyncs across devices, but asks for a lot up front.
No accountUseful in under a minute.

Why: people are wary of typing in financial info, and most first-time visitors leave if they have to sign up first. I’d add accounts once people are coming back.

Ranking

Fewest miles firstSimple, but the top result might be something you can’t afford.
Trips you can book firstThe top answer is always one you can act on today.

Why: the cheapest option doesn’t help if you don’t have the points for it.

Scope

Add hotels, multi-city, cash pricesMore to offer, but a lot more to get right.
Just flights, done wellSmaller, but correct.

Why: the first version showed me that doing a lot badly loses trust fast.

06 · What I learned

A few things I’d tell myself at the start.

Trust comes before features

One fake flight did more damage than any missing feature. Checking the data was the most important work I did.

Test it like a stranger would

Going through my own app as a skeptical user found problems I’d walked past dozens of times.

Cutting scope is a decision

Saying no to hotels and cash prices is what made the flight answers good enough to rely on.

07 · Adding an assistant

A small helper for quick questions, built to cost nothing.

Some questions don’t fit a map or a list, like “where can my Chase points go?” or “what does cents per point mean?”. So I added Miles, a small chat assistant in the corner of the app. It can see your balances and the trips from your airport, so its answers are about your wallet, not points in general.

Which AI to use

A top paid modelBest answers, but every question costs a cent or two, paid by me.
A free tier (Groq)No cost, about 100 questions a day. Answers are a bit weaker.

Why: it’s a side project with no revenue, so a bill that grows with every visitor didn’t make sense. I checked Gemini first, but its free tier had been cut to a few requests a day. Groq gave the most free room. The code can switch to a paid model with one setting if it ever needs better answers.

Fit the limit

Send less

Free tiers count every word. I cut what each question sends to the top 15 trips and only your cards’ partners, about a third of the first version.

Stay honest

Answer from real data

It’s told to only use the app’s numbers for your trips, never make up routes or prices, and remind you prices are estimates.

Fail gently

No dead ends

If the daily limit runs out or something breaks, it says so in plain words instead of showing an error.

What I’d measure: how many people open it, how many ask a second question, and whether people who use it save more trips.
