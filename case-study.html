01 · The problem

Transferable points are flexible, and that flexibility is the problem.

Chase, Amex, Capital One, Citi and Bilt points can each move into a dozen or more airline programs, at different ratios, with bonuses that come and go every few weeks. The same flight can cost 52,500 miles in one program and 88,000 in another. To answer “where can I go?” a traveler has to cross-reference transfer charts, award charts, airline partnerships and live seat availability, usually across five browser tabs and a spreadsheet.

Primary user

The multi-card traveler

Holds two or more transferable currencies, takes one to three award trips a year, and knows points are “worth more than cash back” but not how to prove it for a specific trip.

Job to be done

“Show me where my points can take me, and the cheapest way to pay.”

Existing tools answer narrower questions: award search assumes you already know the program; issuer portals only price their own currency; blogs describe sweet spots, not your wallet.

The gap isn’t information. It’s combining your wallet with the rules into one decision.

02 · Auditing my own v1

v1 looked finished. Checked against the real rules, it wasn’t trustworthy.

Before adding features I tested v1 as a skeptical user would: checked every airline and transfer partner against the current published charts, and walked the onboarding end to end. In a trust-sensitive product, one wrong answer costs you the user, so accuracy came before new features.

04 · Key decisions & tradeoffs

What I chose, what I gave up, and why.

Pricing data

Live award search for every routeAccurate, but slow, rate-limited and costly per visitor.
Chart estimates + live check on demandInstant browsing; real seats fetched only when a user signals intent.

Why: browsing needs speed, booking needs truth. Spending live queries only at the moment of intent keeps one API key inside its daily limit, and the UI labels every estimate as one.

Accounts

Sign-up to save a walletEnables sync; adds friction before any value.
No account; data stays in the browserValue in under a minute; nothing sensitive leaves the device.

Why: balances are personal financial data, and time-to-value decides whether a first-time visitor stays. Accounts return only once saved trips prove people come back.

API keys

Browser calls the data providerSimple, but exposes keys and is blocked by CORS.
Server-side proxy with edge cachingKey never ships to the client; repeat searches are free.

Why: security and cost. A two-hour edge cache means ten people checking Seattle → Tokyo cost one upstream call.

Ranking

Cheapest miles firstEasy to explain, but ignores whether the user can actually pay.
Bookable first, then fewest of your pointsThe top answer is always one you can act on today.

Why: an unaffordable “best” option is noise. The ranking answers the user’s real question, not the chart’s.

Scope cut

Hotels, multi-city, cash pricesBroader, but each doubles the data problem.
One-way & round-trip flights from five hubs, done rightNarrow and correct beats wide and wrong.

Why: v1 showed breadth without accuracy destroys trust. Depth first, then expand.

06 · Architecture

Simple on purpose.

A single static page does all pricing in the browser, so it’s instant and free to host. Serverless functions exist only where a secret or a cache is needed.
